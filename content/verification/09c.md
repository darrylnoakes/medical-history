# Verification of batch 09c

Second-pass fact-check. Sources were re-fetched (PMC via Europe PMC/eutils, FDA, LOC) and quotes and claims compared sentence by sentence. Commons file pages were checked for the four images.

## kraepelin — corrected
- Body said dementia praecox had "a steady downhill course"; Hoff says only that Kraepelin saw it as having "a poor prognosis". Changed. The better outlook of manic-depressive insanity is now attributed to Kraepelin's own judgment ("which he judged to carry").
- "Today's diagnostic manuals still follow his lead" went beyond Hoff, who says the categories "we use today" are "more or less Kraepelinian". Reworded to "still recognizably Kraepelinian".
- Link note to *asylum-era* claimed his case records "came from asylum patients"; no source says so (Hoff places him in university clinics at Dorpat, Heidelberg and Munich). Note reworded to the method (long-term observation).
- Residual doubt: "thousands of patients" rests on the 8,000-patient figure that Kalk and Young give for his mood-disorder data. The "borrowed from Morel" claim is sourced, but Morel meant something different by the term.

## freud — corrected
- Body and note said *The Interpretation of Dreams* appeared "at the turn of 1899 and 1900". No source gives a release date; the Cureus paper says 1899, IEP says 1900, and the Deuticke imprint is 1900. Rewritten to say sources differ.
- "The first systematic theory and technique of psychotherapy" was an unqualified first. The source says "first major systematic form of psychotherapy". Now attributed as "often described as".
- Added Freud's life dates (1856–1939, from the IEP title).
- The LOC source was titled for the wrong exhibit page; the page at freud02 is "The Individual: Therapy". Title fixed. The Anna O. and "not nearly as successful" quote is on that page and confirmed.
- The myth box is kept; the LOC quote supports the record.
- Residual doubt: Breuer's dates are omitted because no source gives them.

## xrays — corrected (minor)
- Risk sentence said "perhaps a 10–25 percent risk" for 1896. The source says "up to 10–25 %" against 1–2 % in the following years. Reworded to match.
- Confirmed against the sources: Friday 8 November 1895 "late in the afternoon" (Busch; Nobel says "evening"), two meters, nearly six weeks of secrecy, 22 December hand, recipients of the 1 January 1896 prints, Die Presse on 5 January, Canada bullet and Glasgow Royal Infirmary, Edison in March 1896, BMJ burns in April 1896, Goodspeed's coins in 1890, the Tesla lab fire on 13 March 1895, no patent, first Nobel.
- Image file page confirmed (Hand mit Ringen, 22 December 1895, public domain).
- Residual doubt: Busch also documents Philipp Lenard's priority claim, which is the better-known dispute among physicists. The myth box deals only with Tesla, which is the popular version.

## radiotherapy — confirmed
- Voigt (before 3 February 1896, pain relief, inoperable pharyngeal carcinoma), Despeignes (4–23 July 1896, Lyon-trained, later a district physician), Freund (24 November 1896, five-year-old girl) and Grubbe "seems to have been disqualified" are all in the sources. The life dates for Despeignes, Grubbe and Freund are in the cited papers.
- Residual doubt: Cosset calls Despeignes "the most serious candidate" for first radiation therapist, and the body does not rank the claimants. That is deliberate, given the dispute. The rating of the claim as `disputed` stands.

## aspirin — corrected
- Body stated flatly that Eichengrün "directed Hoffmann's work"; that is a claim in Wood's commentary, not settled fact. Reworded as a description by Wood. Wood says Eichengrün "claimed" his contribution was expunged and that Sneader supports this view; the text now attributes it that way, and the confidence note says Sneader is cited via Wood.
- Myth record said the father story "is a legend told since the 1930s". The Science History Institute calls it a legend, but the "since the 1930s" refers to the omission of Eichengrün, not the father story. Wood calls the father story "the accepted story". Separated and attributed.
- Removed "(then Elberfeld)" from the place; no source names Elberfeld (the 125th-anniversary paper says Wuppertal).
- Confirmed: Stone's Chipping Norton and about-50-patients details and the search finding "references to the tree's name", the 10 August 1897 quote, the 1874 salicylic acid and stomach irritation, the heroin two-week window, and the 6 March 1899 trademark.
- Image: Commons describes "Bottle with aspirin, 1899, from Bayer Archives" (CC BY-SA 3.0); the caption is acceptable.
- Residual doubt: Stone's cures are his own claim; Wood notes that some patients recovered only partly, and his cases may have been malaria.

## insect-vectors — corrected (minor)
- Link to *nuremberg-code* was `led-to`, implying the Reed contracts caused Nuremberg; changed to `see-also`.
- Confirmed in Cox, Pozzi and Riva, and the challenge-studies chapter: Manson 1877, Finlay's failure from too short an interval, Ross's "Mosquito day" of 20 August 1897, dappled-winged (anopheline) mosquitoes, bird malaria in Calcutta with grey (culicine) mosquitoes, "several thousand mosquitoes", Grassi's 1 November 1898 volunteer, the rivalry over the 1902 Nobel, and the $100 contract doubled to $200 for those who fell ill.
- Residual doubt: the "prototype of informed consent" is described by the source as something Reed is "frequently credited" with. The body uses the hedged wording.

## viruses-discovered — confirmed
- All three sources support the 1892 Ivanovsky filtration, Beijerinck's 1898 *contagium vivum fluidum* and the name "virus", and Loeffler and Frosch's particulate conclusion on foot-and-mouth disease. Zerbini and Kitajima say the "fluid" idea was wrong and that the nature of viruses was elucidated in the 1930s.
- Residual doubt: Ivanovsky himself did not conclude that he had found a new kind of agent; the body only says what he observed.

## patent-medicines — corrected
- Summary said these products "were legal until 1906", implying a ban. The 1906 act required labeling only and did not outlaw heroin or Microbe Killer. Reworded to "sold with no federal oversight until 1906".
- Title "the first drug laws" was an unearned first. Retitled "Patent medicines and the 1906 Food and Drugs Act".
- Confirmed in the FDA pages: signing on 30 June 1906, Adams, eleven listed ingredients including alcohol, heroin and cocaine, Microbe Killer, and the Supreme Court ruling in *U.S. v. Johnson* (1911). The Science History Institute supports Bayer's sale of heroin as a cough suppressant, and Pinho supports the 1898 launch.
- Residual doubt: none material.
