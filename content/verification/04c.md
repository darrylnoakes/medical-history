- Summary said the doctors "found it right where Chinese theory was wrong". The NLM source says only that the book "called into question" kanpō anatomical theory. Summary and body reworded to match.
- Body said "eleven drafts" and "classical Chinese script". The National Diet Library page says "11 revisions" and "a style of Japanese using only classical Chinese characters". Changed.

## hanaoka-seishu (tier 2) — substantially rewritten

- Name: the body said Hanaoka devised *tsūsensan*. A Masui (2015) paper reports that Hanaoka and his pupils wrote *mafutsusan*, and that "tsūsensan" appears in Edo-period documents only on his epitaph. Body, people and summary now use *mafutsusan* and explain the other name.
- Secrecy: the third paragraph stated as fact that Hanaoka made students keep the formula secret and that this kept the method in Japan. A 2017 *Journal of Anesthesia History* review says "Despite common belief, Hanaoka was not secretive about his technique", and Masui (2016) lists the secrecy story among "misunderstandings". Another paper (Imamura, 1862) says disciples were "sternly asked not to leak" the prescription, and one printed it in 1862. The paragraph now states the dispute and both sides.
- Date: all accounts consulted give 13 October 1804, and Japanese sources give it as "Bunka 1, 10th month, 13th day" style dates. Japan then used a lunisolar calendar, so the Western-calendar day may be a conversion or a carry-over of the Japanese month and day. No source we read says which. The confidence note now says so; date stays `agreed`.
- Kamada's textbook: sources differ (written 1839, published 1840 per different accounts); body now says "around 1840".
- Hekint International essay (by a high-school student) was typed `scholarly`; changed to `reference`. It is still used for the alkaloids, dog trials and "over 150" figure; the 2004 *Breast Cancer* abstract (156 operations, 38 years before Long's trial) now backs the number independently.
- Four peer-reviewed abstracts added (see the source list). They are cited through Europe PMC's API record URLs because PubMed, ScienceDirect and Springer block the checker.
- Image: Commons file is catalogued as from Hanaoka's "Surgical Casebook" (NLM Turning the Pages). Caption left as "a surgical casebook of Hanaoka Seishū's school".
- Residual doubt: whether the 1804 operation was a lumpectomy or partial mastectomy (sources differ); the "earliest well-documented" wording rests on historians' judgment, not on a surviving contemporaneous record.

## tcm-standardised (tier 3) — substantially rewritten

- The entry leaned on a magazine (Asimov Press) and a polemical Slate essay. Slate removed. Added three peer-reviewed sources: Gross (*Medical History* 2018), Xu and Yang (*Health Policy*), and Tu Youyou's Nobel biography as a primary source.
- Error: body said scientists "were sent on three-year courses". Tu's own account says her program was about two and a half years (1959 to 1962). Fixed.
- Removed the sentence that the term "traditional Chinese medicine" first appeared in English. It rested only on Levinovitz in Slate and is not supported by a scholarly source we could open.
- Removed the "Mao in 1950 noted 500 million people" sentence (Slate only); replaced with the 1955 head counts from Gross (about 70,000 Western-trained doctors, 360,000 Chinese-medicine practitioners).
- Myth record and confidence note now mention that Republican-era reformers had already begun modernizing Chinese medicine (Gross), so "assembled in the 1950s" is not a creation from nothing.
- Summary rewritten; it claimed the system "taught worldwide today took shape in 1950s Beijing", which overreaches.
- Link to `artemisinin` changed from `led-to` to `see-also`; Tu's training was a 1959 to 1962 state course, and "shaped her search of old texts" was inference.
- The plan row mentions Mao's 1958 "treasure house" line and state colleges in 1956; neither was sourced, so they are not in the entry.
- Residual doubt: how much was "invented" versus continued is a matter of emphasis among historians. 1954 reversal rests on Asimov Press (a historian's essay in a magazine), corroborated only indirectly by Gross's citations to Mao's 1954 directives.

## barefoot-doctors (tier 2) — corrected

- Summary said they "became a model for primary care worldwide"; sources say WHO and some countries studied the program. Softened.
- Body said the Alma-Ata Declaration was "built on community workers and traditional practitioners". The declaration says only that primary care relies on such workers; it does not mention China. Now says one review (Lee and Kim, 2018) holds that the program "greatly influenced" it.
- "Many recruits were poorly trained" was not in the sources. Replaced with Gross's finding that some barefoot doctors treated complicated diseases instead of referring them.
- Fall in infectious disease: NPR attributes it to China generally; the body no longer implies barefoot doctors alone achieved it.
- Date: the program had precursors (grassroots health workers in the 1950s per Gross; commune health stations from 1958 per Chineseposters.net). Body and note say the 1965 start is a convention; earliest stays 1965.
- Link note to `alma-ata` softened to match the evidence.
- Residual doubt: Chineseposters.net is a `reference`; the only other news source is NPR 2005. The 15 percent figure is Mao's own claim as reported.
- Image: not re-checked against Commons description beyond the file title (API rate-limited); see end note.

- Checked every laboratory date, number and quotation against Tu Youyou's Nobel biography: 2,000 recipes to 640 prescriptions, sample 191 on 4 October 1971, July 1972 self-test, 21 patients in Hainan (August to October 1972), crystals November 1972, and the March 1972 report in Nanjing. All match.
- "Mice" changed to "rodents" (Tu says rodent malaria). Project 523 "meeting held on 23 May 1967" changed to a meeting that "opened" on 23 May; Asimov Press gives 23 to 30 May.
- WHO: the body said WHO recommended combination therapy "only in 2005". That is Asimov Press's date; we could not open a WHO source to confirm it and WHO publications earlier in the 2000s discuss combinations. Reworded to "much later (one history dates it to 2005)".
- Image caption said the Elbe valley "Germany"; Commons gives only "Elbe-Tal, 9 September 2007". "Germany" removed.
- Link note to `tcm-standardised` corrected (Tu's course was 1959 to 1962, not the early 1950s).
- Residual doubt: date confidence stays `firm` for the year-level date, but exact steps rest on Tu's 2015 recollection plus later published project records, not contemporaneous documents we could open. Credit is genuinely contested (note and myth box say so). The Ge Hong date "340 CE" comes from the Nobel background paper; his life dates (284 to 363) from JCI.
