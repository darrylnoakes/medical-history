# Verification — batch 05a (Islamic stream)

Second-pass fact-check of eight entries. Every source URL was re-fetched and each quote and "supports" claim read in context. Result: 0 entries confirmed unchanged, 6 corrected, 2 substantially rewritten (gondishapur, islamic-pharmacy).

## gondishapur — substantially rewritten
- Date data: anchor was 550 with a 260–765 range and display "c. 6th century (traditional)", which mixed the city's founding with the legend. Re-anchored on the documented founding (260), kept the 260–765 smear, and added `date.traditional` (c. 555, Khosrow I) from Miller's "about 555 AD", so the legend draws as a ghost. Display now says which date is which. (Validator warns that year 260 differs from the plan's 550; this is deliberate.)
- Myth "record" said "No contemporary Persian or Greek source describes such an academy" and that a hospital is "well attested". Neither is in the sources: Taylor says only that contemporary texts are scarce and the sources were written much later, and Nayernouri argues from earlier *Syriac* Christian texts that a school existed. Rewrote to say what the sources say, including Nayernouri's counter-case.
- Body: "family myth; others defend it" made specific (Western sceptics versus Nayernouri's Syriac-text argument). Prisoners "captured from the Romans in 260" now says "taken in his victory over Valerian in 260" (Taylor); Nayernouri gives 256–260, noted in the source's `supports`.
- Link note to translation-movement claimed Gondishapur families "trained Baghdad's translators". The sources support only that the Bakhtishus and Ibn Masawayh moved to Baghdad and that Ibn Masawayh taught Hunayn (GEDSH, DSB). Corrected.
- Residual doubt: sources disagree on whether Jibrail ibn Bakhtishu was Jurjis's son (Miller) or grandson (Taylor, Nayernouri); the entry avoids the question.

## translation-movement — corrected
- "Al-Mansur's secretary Ibn al-Muqaffa' translated from Middle Persian" overstated. SEP says the logic compendium attributed to him may be by his son (under al-Ma'mun), and that only if the father wrote it was the source Middle Persian. Reworded and flagged the dispute.
- "A 9th-century hospital formulary from Gondishapur mixed Greek, Indian and Persian ingredients in single recipes": Taylor says only that Sabur's formulary shows a synthesis of Persian, Greek and Indian medicine (and that it served as a hospital formulary in Baghdad). Reworded to that.
- Date end 1000 and the claim of "fading after about two centuries" were unsupported (and inconsistent: two centuries is about 950). SEP says translations blossomed in the first two Abbasid centuries. End set to 950, display "c. 750–950", note rewritten.
- "Rich officials" corrected to "wealthy lords" (DSB). Myth box: removed the unsourced reign dates of al-Ma'mun; "historians doubt" replaced by SEP's wording ("cannot be viewed as an institutional center").
- Confirmed: Hunayn's dates, father a pharmacist, teacher Ibn Masawayh, circle (son Ishaq, nephew Hubaysh), "well over a hundred" works, the manuscript-search letter, sense-for-sense method, creation of scientific Arabic; al-Mansur and 762; Sanskrit fragments in the *Hawi* (Kahl).
- No image found that I could verify on Commons (searches for Hunayn returned nothing usable); none added.
- Residual doubt: "almost all of Galen" is GEDSH's wording for Hunayn *and his school*.

## bimaristans — corrected
- Date anchor 805 vs display "c. 800": set to 800 (range 786–809 kept).
- Myth "tradition" was uncited. Miller (2006) is exactly the claim (medical records kept by students, licensing boards, academic medical centers); added him as the tradition-side source and reworded the tradition to match.
- The 12th-century Nur al-Din revival was credited to a Northrup quote that did not contain it. Added a second Northrup quote (Tabbaa on a twelfth-century revival of medicine).
- Etymology "sick person"/"place" confirmed (NLM brochure: "ill person"); waqf property, women's hall, wards, pharmacy, library, 872 Ibn Tulun, 982 'Adudi (Miller says 981; body says "981 or 982"), Ali ibn Isa and the humoral, non-religious medicine all confirmed.
- Residual doubt: the founder of Baghdad's first hospital (Harun or Yahya ibn Khalid) and date (c. 800) rest on later biographers; Miller names Jibrail as head but calls him Jurjis's son (see gondishapur).

## islamic-pharmacy — substantially rewritten
- The title and body asserted that the druggist "was a recognized trade" and that market inspectors checked drugs in the 9th century. The sources show individual druggists and formularies in the 9th century, but the inspector evidence is only that manuals "prior to the 12th century" mentioned the medical profession "briefly, mostly in relation to drugs, weights, and measures". Retitled ("The druggist's trade takes shape"), rewrote summary, body and note accordingly.
- "Formularies drawing on camphor and musk" separated: NLM says such drugs were in use by the 9th century, not that formularies were built on them. "Around 1050" for al-Biruni: DSB says he was past 80 when writing (b. 973), death "after 1050"; kept as approximate.
- Residual doubt: whether pharmacy "became a profession" at any datable moment is not established; the date range 800–1050 is wide on purpose.

## al-zahrawi — corrected
- Body said "tartar causes gum disease and how to scrape it away"; the cited page does not say this and Leclerc's text describes scraping deposits but not "tartar" as a cause. Replaced with the sourced point (importance for dentistry; one of the earliest discussions of tooth prostheses, GMN).
- "Leclerc found him writing *again and again* from his own practice": Leclerc says he is "less an imitator" and speaks "more often" from experience. Reworded. Leclerc's dates and "first translation into French" were unsourced; added GMN record.
- Dates conflict not disclosed: the NLM brochure puts him in Cordoba under 'Abd al-Rahman III (912–961); the catalogue gives 936–1013 and c. 1000 for the *Tasrif*. Note now states the conflict. Date range 950–1013 kept.
- Link kinds: guy-de-chauliac "led-to" was wrong (the 1500 printing merely shared a volume) -> see-also; sabuncuoglu "Ottoman illustrated surgery drew on the same tradition" is unsourced -> softened to see-also.
- No image added: Wellcome "Chirurgia" plates on Commons do not say which edition they come from; skipped rather than guess.
- Residual doubt: "first to describe instruments with figures" is Leclerc's 1861 judgment (with his own hedge), not settled scholarship; the body attributes it to him.
