# Verification 03a (India: Atharvaveda to Vagbhata)

All 21 original quotes were found on their pages. The problems were in what the text claimed beyond them.

## atharvaveda-healing — corrected
- Summary said "India's oldest medical words", "flatter, bribe": overreach (Rigveda also has healing hymns; nothing in Bloomfield about bribing). Softened to "among the oldest" and "pay homage ... and send it packing".
- Body said healers "begged it to go to foreign peoples"; Bloomfield (V.22.14) says takman is "delivered over" to the Gandharis, Mujavants, Angas and Magadhas. Reworded and named two.
- Date range began at 1200 BCE, but the only dating source (Jamison and Witzel) puts the Atharvaveda after c. 1100 BCE. Earliest changed to -1100; note now says the 800 BCE end is a conventional estimate and hymns may predate the collection.
- Residual doubt: takman = malaria is Bloomfield's 1897 interpretation, flagged in the text.

## charaka-samhita — corrected
- "Oldest surviving complete treatise": Wujastyk says Caraka, Sushruta and Bhela are "the oldest Indian medical texts we have". Changed to "one of the oldest surviving".
- Agnivesha was labeled "legendary"; sources only say an early text by him was edited. Relabeled.
- Debate was about the cornerstone of "medicine", not "health"; fixed. Wujastyk's wording on the Atharvaveda advice is "slightly knowing ... didactic strategy", not "diplomacy"; fixed. Pedigree-as-literary-frame is Wujastyk's reading; now attributed.
- `confidence.date` agreed -> uncertain: a 300-year window resting on internal philosophical evidence, in a text built by three hands.
- Body was trimmed slightly; myth box kept (record is sourced).
- Residual doubt: the Tricycle review (typed news) is the only source for Zysk's thesis, a secondary report of a 1991 book.

## sushruta-samhita — corrected
- Couching claims ("without anesthesia", "poor vision in an age without spectacles", "better than blindness") appeared in no cited source; the AAO page only says "Couching Needle" and gives the 600 BCE date. Cut them and added Raju and Raju (IJO 2017, PMC5742952) for a plain definition of couching.
- "Brickmaker caste, unlikely to have had access to manuscripts" is the Wire author's argument, not Wujastyk's; now attributed to "a recent analysis".
- Nose-repair details (leaf template, cheek flap, bandage, two pipes), 878 CE manuscript, hora and second-century astrology, "brief and laconic" all confirmed. Image file and caption match LACMA metadata.
- Residual doubt: the date range (-100 to 500) is a scholar's window from a news article plus the hora passage; the AAO label's 600 BCE is shown as the traditional date.

## ashoka-edicts — corrected
- Note claimed the edict "dates itself to Ashoka's twelfth regnal year". Rock Edict II carries no such statement; that line belongs to RE III. Dhammika dates the Girnar version of RE II to 257 BCE (and other versions to 256). Note rewritten.
- "Ashoka (d. 232 BCE)": no cited source. Replaced with "born c. 304 BCE" (Dhammika, who also says his dates are disputed).
- "names no buildings at all" was false (wells are mentioned); now "no hospital buildings". Myth record likewise.
- Residual doubt: 257 BCE depends on Mauryan chronology.

## bower-manuscript — corrected
- Confidence note said Hoernle first dated it to the mid-fifth century and later pushed earlier. Hoernle's 1891 note says not later than about 500 CE; his 1912 introduction dates older parts to the fourth century. Note and `supports` fixed.
- The scribe's salutation shows the copyist was Buddhist, not necessarily the author; supports line now says so (the body already said "copied").
- Link note claimed it "parallels" Charaka; Wujastyk says such manuscripts differ from the classical compendia (little theory). Note reworded.
- Summary "oldest Indian medical writing on record" softened to "among the oldest surviving Indian manuscripts".
- Image caption: Bodleian location rests on Hoernle's 1912 introduction; now attributed.
- Residual doubt: Hoernle's own text records disputes about exactly which stupa (Qum Tura vs Qutluq Urda) yielded it.

## vagbhata — corrected
- Summary ("textbook much of Asia would learn from") overreached; now follows Wujastyk's "one of the most important medical treatises in Asia". Removed "brilliantly" (hype).
- Body implied the *Ashtangahridaya* itself traveled to Tibet; the source says Ayurvedic works in general did. Reworded, as was the link note.
- "Some historians think Vagbhata may have been a Buddhist" -> Wujastyk thinks it possible.
- Residual doubt: the IIAS PDF text is garbled for Sanskrit names; the title *Ashtangahridaya* was confirmed in the Blackwell Companion chapter's abbreviations list.
