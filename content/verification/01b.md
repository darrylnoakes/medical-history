# Verification, batch 01b

Second-pass fact-check. Every source was re-fetched and the quotes and supporting context read. Validator: 0 errors; two length warnings remain (sakikku and imhotep run 213 and 218 words against a 210 cap). Source checker: all sources `ok`.

## sakikku: corrected
- Summary said the handbook "named which god, demon or ghost was behind each illness". Geller, the cited source, reads the "hand of a god" notation as an association, not a cause, and says Nils Heeßel disagrees. The summary now says "many entries link an illness to a god, demon or ghost", and the body states both views.
- Body said the omens were twisted threads "in his words" with "no master copy". The sources (Jiménez, Geller) give a catalogue rubric credited to him, with "no copy (GABA.RI) existed". Reworded as "credited by a later catalogue note".
- "Famous name became the label for a corpus far too large for one person" is not in Geller. Replaced with what he does say: a single name lending authority to standardized texts, with the Hippocratic name as a "brand name" for a corpus mostly by anonymous writers.
- "Probably" added to the pig/brick/pregnant-woman omen, as in the source. The claim that treatment lived in separate texts is now tied to Geller's point that therapeutic texts rarely give prognoses.
- Link note on ashurbanipal-library softened. The sources show an editorial method credited to Esagil-kin-apli, not that Assyrian editions were built on these particular catalogues.
- Residual doubt: the 11th-century date and the whole attribution rest on late catalogue notes. Geller says only that he is "said to have lived" then.

## ashurbanipal-library: corrected
- "More than 13,000 lines" is a reconstruction. Simkó's table gives an original count of about 13,253 lines, of which 5,757 survive (43 percent). Body, summary and note now say "estimated".
- "For generations the tablets looked random" softened to "long looked", which is what the sources support.
- Changed "encyclopaedia" to "encyclopedia" as a common noun (American spelling); the proper name is kept.
- Link note to sakikku softened, for the same reason as above.
- Residual doubt: the reign dates (669–c. 630) are fine, but the tablets could be copied late in the reign or slightly after.

## imhotep: corrected
- People line said Osler "popularized Imhotep as a doctor". No source says so, and Blomstedt blames Waddell's later Manetho translation. Reworded to what Osler actually said.
- Sources disagree about Manetho. Escolano-Poveda (ARCE) says Manetho describes Imhotep as devoted to medicine. Blomstedt, citing Wildung and Adler, says Manetho names only the king Tosorthros and that the name Imhotep was inserted by Waddell (and earlier by Maspero). The body and note now say so; before, only Blomstedt's view was given.
- "The first link to healing appears only in the 4th century BCE" became "earliest surviving health-related reference". That is Blomstedt's phrasing, and ARCE's Manetho reading would put an earlier link in the 3rd century BCE.
- Added the Sainsbury Centre's statement that no evidence from Djoser's time ties him to the Step Pyramid. The ARCE inference from his titles is kept.
- Osler lectures were given April 1913 and published 1921 (Yale). Noted in the source `supports`. Summary changed from "thousands of years" to "more than two thousand years".
- Image checked. The Commons page confirms a Louvre figurine, E 4216, dated 332–30 BCE, so the caption is right.
- Residual doubt: dates for Djoser's reign vary by decades (ARCE: 2686–2613 BCE).

- Iry "of roughly 2500 BCE" was not in the sources. Breasted says only that he lived about eight hundred years before the 17th-century copy of the Edwin Smith Papyrus. The date display is now a range, c. 2650–2300 BCE, with `uncertain` kept.
- Myth record said "no Old Kingdom healer of that name is attested". Newsweek reports that the name Merit Ptah does occur, as the wife of Ramose in a New Kingdom tomb, and on no list of healers. Record rewritten. The Peseshet link is now attributed to Kwiecinski ("argues"), not stated as fact. Summary "never was" became "never existed [as a physician]".
- Link note on edwin-smith-papyrus fixed. The papyrus copy was made centuries later, not "written down" then.
- Residual doubt: Peseshet's and Iry's exact dynasties are not given in the sources I could open. Whether Hesy-Ra was a physician at all is open.
