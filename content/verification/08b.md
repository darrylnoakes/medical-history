## heroic-medicine: corrected
- Body said Washington's illness "got the same 'heroic' bloodletting and blistering" as Rush's. The source only says he was bled and blistered after a sore throat. Reworded to say that, without claiming the same regimen.
- Confidence note said historians since had judged Rush's treatment harmful. Neither the Penn nor the Encyclopedia.com text says that. Both report only the charge by colleagues and critics, so the note now says "the charge of some colleagues then".
- "No records" now reads "no records that could test it". That matches Encyclopedia.com's wording. Rush's own account does contain cases.
- Confirmed: 4,000–5,000 dead (about a tenth of the city), August–November 1793, Rush stayed, the excited-vessel theory, "seventy and eighty ounces in five days" and calomel (all in Rush's own 1794 text).
- Residual doubt: the date range 1793–1799 is an illustrative span. "Heroic medicine" as a period label ran much longer, and the Encyclopedia.com source supplies the label only in passing.

## pinel-moral-treatment: substantially rewritten
- The writer could not source who removed the chains and when. I found Dora Weiner's 1992 *American Journal of Psychiatry* paper (abstract via Europe PMC and efetch). It says Pussin removed the chains from the men at Bicêtre in 1797, replacing them with straitjackets, and that Pinel followed at the Salpêtrière three years later. Her 1979 paper on the document found in 1978 says Pussin initiated Pinel into moral methods. I added the 1992 paper as a source with a `manualCheck`: PubMed and the publisher block bots.
- Changed `date.year` from 1795 to 1797. Added `date.traditional` at 1793, the year the legend and the NLM text give. Claim confidence changed from `disputed` to `agreed`. The note says the scholarly view rests chiefly on Weiner.
- The old text implied Pinel's book names Pussin as the praised "keeper". In the quoted passage the keeper is unnamed. Pussin is named as governor in case histories elsewhere in the book. The body and `supports` now say this.
- "Moved to the Salpêtrière" is now "became chief physician", per the Dictionary of Scientific Biography.
- Image: Commons gives only "Pinel freeing the insane from their chains", Tony Robert-Fleury, 1870s. The caption's "oil study" and "pale young woman" were not supported. Rewrote the caption and alt text, and "fixed the legend" is now "helped fix".
- Rush link note now matches the Penn text: his theory ascribed insanity too to excited vessels needing depletion.
- Noted but not used: the NLM page says Pinel unchained "lunatic women" at Bicêtre. Bicêtre held men and the Salpêtrière held women, so the NLM wording is itself loose.
- Residual doubt: I could read only Weiner's abstracts, not the full papers. The exact numbers freed and the timing at the Salpêtrière (about 1800) come from the abstract alone.
