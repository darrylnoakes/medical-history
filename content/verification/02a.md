# Verification of batch 02a

Method: every source re-read from saved copies of the pages; quotes machine-checked (all pass except the PMC caduceus article, which returned HTTP 429/500 from PMC and Europe PMC during the run; the quoted sentence was read in the writer's downloaded copy of that page and appears there verbatim). Commons metadata checked for the images.

## asclepius-cult: corrected
- Confidence note called the Athens arrival "a contemporary inscription". The source (J-STAGE abstract) says only that the Telemachos monument "tells a story about the introduction" of the god in 420 BCE, so the note now names the monument and drops "contemporary".
- Summary said "the priests carved"; no source mentions priests as the authors, so it now says "the sanctuary".
- Added to the ToposText support that the page itself dates the two large cure inscriptions to the 4th century BCE (the body's "4th-century" stelae). The Axon abstract's "four stelae", "about seventy tales" and "probably exposed in the stoa-abaton" are as stated. Cure details (empty socket, five-year pregnancy, silver pig) all match Edelstein's translation.
- Replaced the PMC fullTextXML-style URL form with the standard article URL (already standard).
- Residual doubt: the Telemachos monument itself is probably somewhat later than 420 BCE; the body only says "an inscription records", which is safe. No image added (nothing suitable found quickly).

## alcmaeon: corrected
- Date range was too narrow for a note saying dating is argued: Huffman (SEP) says the book was "likely" written 500-450 BCE but cites scholars who put it 470 or later (Guthrie 480-440, Lloyd 490-430), and Année (cited there) proposes the mid-6th century. Range widened to -540..-430; confidence note rewritten to say this (the old note said "c. 500 to after 470", omitting the early and late extremes).
- Summary said "not the heart"; SEP does not say Alcmaeon argued against the heart (Empedocles and Aristotle kept the heart). Summary now says understanding is seated in the brain.
- Myth tradition reworded to what SEP says is actually repeated ("hailed as the first to use dissection"), since "father of anatomy" was not sourced. The record (Calcidius' eye passage mostly describes Herophilus; at most he excised an animal's eye) matches SEP.
- Link to alexandria-dissection was "led-to", implying Alcmaeon enabled Herophilus; sources say only that Herophilus reverted to his view. Changed to see-also. Link to four-humours changed from built-on to see-also (no source says the humoral authors drew on him).
- Residual doubt: "first" to put understanding in the brain is Theophrastus' report via Huffman, not independent proof.

## hippocrates: corrected
- Body said modern scholars "have no sample of his own prose against which to test" the texts. Not stated by the source; the source says attribution has rested on judgments of literary merit and rational content and "has not produced general agreement". Rewritten accordingly.
- Myth record named Alcmaeon as a contemporary sharing natural explanations; the cited sources say the Hippocratic writers were "influenced by the pre-Socratic philosophers". Reworded to that. Link note to alcmaeon adjusted to match.
- Checked and confirmed: 460/370 dates and Larissa (Joly, with "brief and late" Lives), Peloponnesian War peak, Plato's two mentions, ~60 works dated last decades of 5th to first half of 4th century, Alexandria collection, later "chemical, mechanist, vitalist, holistic" Hippocrates and homeopathy, Epidemics I and Sacred Disease quotes.
- Residual doubt: Joly (in the same page) argues that some treatises can be assigned to Hippocrates with fair confidence (Epidemics I and III, Sacred Disease and others are "tempting"); the myth's "none can be securely assigned" follows King's later revision and the NLM. No image added.

## hippocratic-oath: substantially rewritten (date, one unsupported claim)
- Date was given as 5th or 4th century BCE (range -450 to -300) resting on Joly's remark that the Oath "may be slightly earlier". The same Encyclopedia.com page (Ferngren entry) says the earliest mention is 1st century CE, it "may date from as early as the fourth century bce", and Edelstein put its ethical content in the late 4th century or later. Date now "Probably 4th century BCE", year -375, range -450 to -250; note and body state the disagreement; a second source entry (Ferngren, same URL) with a verbatim quote was added.
- Body claimed the Oath "may mark the moment when the Asclepiads of Cos began teaching outsiders". Nothing in the sources says this. Replaced with what Jones's text supports: teaching limited to the swearer's own sons, his teacher's sons and sworn pupils.
- Place "Greece (probably Cos)" had no support; now "Greece". Summary said the Oath bans "the knife"; it forbids only cutting for stone, so summary now lists poison and abortion pessaries instead.
- Added an image (Wellcome papyrus fragment of the Oath, CC BY 4.0). The Commons record gives no date, so the caption makes no claim about age.
- Confirmed: Jones's "unknowable" and "counsel of perfection", Erotian's acceptance, the absence of "First, do no harm" (NLM), Epidemics I maxim, Oath contents.
- Residual doubt: nothing identifies where or by whom it was written.

## four-humours: corrected
- Myth record said ancient testimony attributes the treatise "largely" to Polybus. Aristotle cites Polybus for the vein description (ch. 11) and the Anonymus Londinensis for part of the first section, while attributing another part to Hippocrates (Jones's Loeb introduction). Changed to "in part"; the Polybus people line reworded the same way ("probable author" removed).
- Galen's dates given as 129-216; sources disagree (DSB gives c. 216; another entry 199/200). Written 129-c. 216 CE.
- Last paragraph said only the bacteriological revolution of the late 19th century drove humours out; the Oxford Companion says "bacteriological and technological revolutions ... of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries have all but obliterated" them. Corrected.
- Link to alcmaeon changed from built-on to see-also (influence not sourced).
- Confirmed: quotation from Nature of Man, water-to-black-bile replacement, jaundice/gall bladder rationale, Galen's qualities/elements, 18th-century bleeding, Arabic medicine, Cnidian four-fluid scheme. Image: Commons description (airbrush by Lois Hague, 1991, Wellcome) matches caption.
- Residual doubt: the Anonymus' split attribution means authorship of the treatise is not settled.

## plague-of-athens: corrected (minor)
- "At least 29 diseases" changed to "29 or more", the wording of Olson et al. (they speak of "29 or more prior theories"). Other claims (second year of the war, Thucydides' symptoms and the physicians, as much as a third of the population, 150-body mass grave, Shapiro et al. reply) verified. Sources give 430-427/425 BCE; the 430-c. 426 span is a fair summary.
- Residual doubt: the "third of the population" is a modern estimate cited by Scientific American, not firm.

## alexandria-dissection: corrected
- Body said elsewhere in the Greek world human dissection "was abhorrent". The Herophilus source says only that Alexandria's atmosphere, "possibly unique in Greek cities at that time", did not meet with general disapproval. Reworded.
- Body said Erasistratus "agreed that the nerves start in the brain"; the source says he "was most probably in agreement" with Herophilus. Softened.
- Cut the closing sentence that systematic human dissection did not return "for many centuries"; no cited source supports it (the Galen source supports only that human dissection was no longer possible in his day, and that his anatomy stood until 1543).
- Herophilus' birth now "last third of the 4th century BCE" as in the source.
- Confirmed: Celsus passage and his own verdict ("as cruel as it is needless"), Tertullian's "butcher", scholars divided and Galen's silence (Erasistratus entry), Galen advising Alexandria for skeletons, Herophilus' discoveries, the water-clock pulse as late testimony.
- Residual doubt: whether the vivisection charge is history is unresolved; no image added (the only Commons candidate is a 16th-century imagined woodcut).

## theriac: corrected (minor)
- "The most powerful king of his day" was the writer's own gloss; it now attributes the phrase to Pliny ("the most powerful monarch of that period"). Walnut-fig-rue recipe, Pompey finding it in the king's cabinet, Celsus' 36 ingredients in honey, treacle etymology, trust into the 18th century and the translators' "a mere fable, no doubt" all verified in the Bostock and Riley notes. The Galen link (Marcus Aurelius taking an antidote daily, De antidotis) matches the same notes.
- Residual doubt: the date range is for the legend, which is attested only in Roman writers of the following century; Andromachus' theriac is a 1st-century CE development.

## celsus: corrected
- Display date "c. 25-50 CE" did not match any source; the sources say reign of Tiberius, with records placing him at Rome in 25/26 CE. Now "c. 25 CE (reign of Tiberius)", range unchanged.
- Spencer's introduction notes that recent research makes it likely Celsus lived in Narbonensis, which the entry ignored; body now says "apparently in Rome" and place records the alternative.
- Link to printing-medicine was "led-to" although the Celsus printing (1478) is earlier than the target and did not enable it; changed to see-also with a revised note.
- Confirmed: four signs of inflammation, 49 editions by 1841, lost manuscript copied in the 15th century, eight books, among the first medical books printed, practitioner status disputed.
