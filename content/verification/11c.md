# Verification, batch 11c

## the-pill: corrected
- Checked against the FDA historian's article, both PBS pages, and the Science History Institute biographies: 1957 approval for menstrual disorders, 1960 contraceptive approval, half a million users by late 1959, Sanger and Pincus in 1951, McCormick's $40,000 in 1953, 50-woman Massachusetts trial under a fertility-study cover, April 1956 Río Piedras, Rice-Wray's 100% / 17% report, three uninvestigated deaths, 6.5 million users by 1965, Marker 1942 and Syntex 1944, the 1970 patient leaflet. All confirmed.
- Added life dates for Rock (1890–1984) and Marker (1902–1995), both in the sources.
- "Chief danger ... had not been anticipated by anyone" now says "at the time of approval" and "almost a decade", as the FDA article does. "Doses have since been cut sharply" is backed by PBS (low-dose versions approved in the 1960s; high-dose pills withdrawn at the FDA's urging in 1988).
- Added the PBS point that Pincus and Rock believed they met the ethical standards of the day, so the Puerto Rico paragraph is not one-sided.
- Link notes rewritten: the Nuremberg link no longer claims the trials "barely met" consent rules on paper; it states what the sources show (the women were not told the pill was experimental).
- Trimmed the body to the tier 1 limit.
- Residual doubt: Syntex's norethindrone is the work of Djerassi's group; other chemists on that team (for example Luis Miramontes) are not named in the sources fetched, so no credit is added. The PBS timeline is a journalistic site, but each point used was cross-checked against the FDA or Science History Institute pages.

## thalidomide: corrected
- Confirmed against Vargesson (2015) and the FDA pages: sedative marketing, sample packets, "completely safe", over 10,000 children, infant mortality as high as 40%, no successful prosecution, withdrawal in the UK in November 1961 and elsewhere by 1962, Kelsey's refusal, press coverage and the 1962 amendments, leprosy, myeloma and Brazil.
- Body said "For years no one connected" nerve damage and malformations. The source says only that reports followed "soon after" release. Now "At first no one connected the two."
- Phocomelia description ("hands and feet project almost directly from the shoulders and hips") was not in the source; replaced with Vargesson's definition (long bones shortened or missing, hand plate left in place).
- Link to evidence-based-medicine changed from "led-to" to "see-also": the sources do not show thalidomide led to EBM, only that proof of efficacy became law in 1962. Streptomycin note reworded to what is defensible.
- Added image (PD, FDA photo of Kennedy presenting Kelsey's award, August 1962); the Commons description matches the caption and Vargesson mentions the award.
- Residual doubt: the claim that Lenz and McBride acted "independently" follows Vargesson; the priority between them is argued elsewhere and is not covered by the sources.

## hip-replacement: substantially rewritten
- The Cureus review (Baig et al.) is internally inconsistent: Teflon in 1956, "low-friction arthroplasty in 1958", and "November 1962" for the technique. The original body asserted November 1962 as the date and said the design "had taken the form that made hip replacement routine" (unsupported). The date is now c. 1958–1962 with confidence date lowered from agreed to uncertain, `earliest` 1958, and the body says histories date the steps differently.
- "Lancashire surgeon" in the summary is in neither source; replaced with "English orthopedic surgeon". "Principles still used" softened to "shaped modern hip replacement".
- Confidence note now explains the disagreement and cites Charnley's 1961 Lancet paper, which appears only in the reference list of the 2024 Cureus review.
- Residual doubt: I could not find an open source that pins the first Teflon operation, the switch to polyethylene, and the date of the cemented design. The dates are loose.

## measles-vaccine: corrected
- Body said Hilleman "produced a further attenuated version in 1968"; Tulchinsky says Hilleman and colleagues "developed and further improved" the Edmonston-Enders strain. Reworded. "More than 40 vaccines" restored to "experimental and licensed", as in the source.
- Confidence note claimed credit "belongs mainly to Enders's group" (an interpretation). Replaced with Tulchinsky's own remark that there were "many key players".
- Link "built-on" to polio-vaccine (Enders's culture methods) was not backed by any cited source; changed to "see-also" with the sourced fact (licensed 1955). Wakefield link changed from "led-to" to "see-also".
- Confirmed: Edmonston aged 13, 1963 licence, MMR 1971, Hilleman 1919–2005. Tulchinsky's table lists MMR as 1969; her text and Parums give 1971 (licence), which is kept.
- Residual doubt: an inactivated measles vaccine was also marketed in the 1960s; the entry says nothing about it.

## hospice: corrected
- Body said Saunders gave opioids "on a regular schedule"; the source says only oral administration, "highly unusual", and that doctors held strong opioids worked only by injection and that addiction would follow regular use (Baines, quoted by Metzger). Reworded; "most doctors" became "doctors commonly".
- "First modern hospice" now attributed to the sources ("described as"). The note's claim that "homes for the dying existed long before" had no source and was cut.
- Confirmed: nurse training in the Second World War, social work, medical degree 1957, St Christopher's 1967, "total pain" with four dimensions introduced in 1964.
- Residual doubt: the social-work training is described only loosely in the source ("in the 1950s").

## heart-transplant: substantially rewritten
- Title changed to "first human-to-human heart transplant" (the source's own phrasing; animal-to-human transplants had been tried).
- Body said the South African government "quickly saw" the propaganda value; the sources say only that nationalist politicians exploited Barnard "in the years following" and that the operation mattered more to South Africa because of apartheid. Rewritten.
- "Most heart transplants ... failed early" replaced with figures from Brink: over 100 transplants in 1968, 11% two-year survival in 1968–70.
- Barnard's debts made explicit: Shumway's team (technique in dogs), Hume's Richmond program (immunosuppression). The note's claim that Barnard "did not hide" the debt had no source and was cut. Hume added to the people list.
- Washkansky paragraph now matches Cooper and Brink (misdiagnosed rejection, pneumonia worsened by extra immunosuppression, autopsy without rejection). Summary's "almost hourly" press coverage is Cooper's phrase.
- Added image (Barnard at a symposium in Amsterdam, 6 June 1968, Dutch National Archives, CC BY-SA); the caption states only what the Commons record says. It is a portrait, not a view of the operation, but no good period image of the operation itself was found.
- Residual doubt: Barnard's "opposition" to segregation rests on Brink's phrase "did his best"; Cooper's account does not discuss it.

- "The gut absorbs sodium with glucose, even during severe diarrhea" was not sourced; replaced with Fisher and Parsons and Schultz and Curran (1950s) on coupled sugar and salt absorption. The Calcutta work used intragastric infusion; "by mouth" was dropped for the Calcutta team.

- Alma-Ata link changed from "led-to" to "see-also"; the sources do not connect ORS to Alma-Ata.
- Added a CDC photograph (public domain) of a cholera patient drinking ORS; the caption says it dates from 1992 and does not show the 1968–71 trials.
- Residual doubt: the 1968 Dhaka and Calcutta priority questions (Hirschhorn, Phillips and others) are only touched on; Bangaon outcome numbers come from papers not fetched here.
