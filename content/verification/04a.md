# Verification, batch 04a

## oracle-bone-medicine: corrected
- Confidence note claimed "translations differ over which ancestor was blamed for the king's tooth". No source says this. Replaced with what Cook (debated readings of some body-part terms) and ISAW (inscriptions cannot be diagnosed; skeletons only suggest candidates) do say.
- Summary said "China's oldest writing"; softened to "Some of China's oldest writing" (the body already said "some").
- Image caption said "13th century BCE"; the Commons page says c. 1200 BCE. Caption now says so and states the bone is an example of the genre, since the page does not say this particular inscription is medical.
- Confirmed: ISAW quote and dates (c. 1250–1050 BCE), the toothache series, caries/abscess/periodontal disease at Yinxu; Cook's list of body parts and "affliction"; Wu Ding's dates (1254–1197 BCE); exorcism and sacrifice only.
- Residual doubt: the Eno source is a class reading with a lightly sardonic voice; it supports only that Wu Ding assumed an ancestral cause.

## mawangdui-manuscripts: corrected
- "Family of Li Cang, a local lord" replaced by "son of Li Cang, a local ruler of the Changsha kingdom" (Cook: Li Cang d. 186 BCE, tomb 3 is his thirty-year-old son's; Early China: probably Li Xi, early thirties, bones put him at 30–40).
- "Lancing of swellings" had been attached to the *Recipes*; Cook says it is in the Mawangdui texts generally, with only cautery for vessels. Reworded. "283 remedies for 103 conditions" now "formulas for diseases" (the PMC wording), and "longest text" narrowed to "longest silk text".
- Added the Zhangjiashan date (186 BCE, Cook).
- Image caption said "20th-century reconstruction"; Commons says only "a reconstruction". Now "modern reconstruction".
- Confirmed: burial 168 BCE, about thirty manuscripts, sixteen medical, early 1970s discovery, eleven vessels without acumoxa, no needling, beating-the-spirits incantation, Qin-period copying from script.
- Residual doubt: identification of the occupant is "probable" only. Counts of texts vary by how fragments are grouped.

## huangdi-neijing: corrected, image and traditional date added
- Opening "No book has shaped Chinese medicine more" was unsupported; replaced with "is treated as the founding text" (UNESCO calls it the fundamental text). Removed the repeated claim at the end.
- "The *Suwen* as now known emerged from 11th-century editing": the Unschuld blurb says only that the text as it is known today emerged in the eleventh century. Reworded; Cook's Tang-material view now attributed as what scholars "suggest".
- Myth record: "older than any securely dated version" changed to "older than any surviving copy".
- Added `date.traditional` (c. 2700 BCE) from Forshaw, British Dental Journal 2025, which writes that it is "conventionally dated to circa 2700 BC". I could not find a history-of-science source that states the traditional date. Some other sources give a reign of about 2697–2598 or 2695–2589 BCE for the Yellow Emperor, so the figure is approximate. The note says it is legend, not a date for any text.
- Added a Ming woodcut (Wellcome, CC BY 4.0; 1591 edition of *Buyi Lei Gong paozhi bianlan*, Yellow Emperor giving books to Lei Gong, inspired by a *Suwen* passage); caption says it is an artist's picture.
- Europe PMC URL changed to the pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov form.
- Confirmed: two books, Qi Bo, question-and-answer form, perverse qi, Warring States dating with Qin and Han additions (UNESCO), listed in Han bibliographies, Unschuld's origin in short essays on vessel theory, needling a minority tradition until the 11th/12th century, 1339 edition.
- Residual doubt: the date range (-300 to 220) cannot cover the view that the received text is mostly Tang-Song; the display says "reshaped to the 11th century". "Hours" among the correspondences rests on Cook's general statement about time numerology, not on a Neijing-specific passage.

## shennong-bencao: corrected, image and traditional date added
- The body stated one dating as agreed, but the sources disagree: Unschuld says 1st century BCE to 1st century CE, the BMC survey says Eastern Han (25–220 CE). Both are now stated in the body.
- Summary "first book of drugs" changed to "earliest known"; "tasted every plant" changed to "said to have".
- Link note claimed "the Tang state revised Tao Hongjing's version in 659", which no source in this entry supports. Made generic.
- Added `date.traditional` (c. 2737 BCE) from Xie et al., Horticulture Research 2023 ("said to have originated from the emperor Chen Nung (2737 BCE)"). This is a plant-science review and weak on history, and it also claims the book was assembled by 221 BCE, which I did not adopt. The 2737 figure is a modern gloss on legend and the note says so. I found no better source in reach.
- Added a Ming woodcut of Shen Nong (Wellcome, CC BY 4.0).
- Confirmed (Unschuld excerpt): three ranks and their poison rule, 120/120/125 by preface and 365 total, Tao Hongjing (452–536) kept three chapters, added 365 records and produced a 730-substance commentary c. 500 CE, modern reconstructions since the 17th century, Huainanzi story of tasting 100 poisons a day.
- Residual doubt: the Tao Hongjing birth year is given as 452 (Unschuld) or 456 (Cook).

## hua-tuo: corrected, image added
- "China's most famous ancient surgeon" is not in any source (Cook: "a healer known for his early surgical techniques"); softened.
- Body said the histories say the powder was "dissolved in wine"; the Ara article says only that he "would have" dissolved it in wine. Now stated as presumed, and cut from the summary. "Including on the internal organs" is now "tradition adds" (ACS Bulletin: "Tradition holds ... visceral surgery").

- Added a Ming woodcut portrait (Wellcome, CC BY 4.0).
