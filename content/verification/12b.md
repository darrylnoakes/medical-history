# Verification, batch 12b

## keyhole-surgery: corrected
- Confidence note said "historians now agree" Mühe came first. Only the two histories cited (Litynski 1998, Polychronidis 2008) were read, so the note now says the priority is stated by those two and that Mouret-first accounts are still common; claim stays `agreed`.
- "Refined and promoted" credited to Dubois and Perissat overreached: the source says Dubois experimented on animals and Perissat improved the technique. Now "helped refine and promote".
- "Displaced open surgery as the standard within a few years" is now attributed ("by one account", within two or three years, gold standard), as in Polychronidis.
- Removed Semm's life dates (1927–2003): no cited source gives them. Image alt/caption trimmed to what the Commons description says (US Navy photo, Peltier Hospital, Djibouti City, 17 Nov 2008; public domain); "watch a video monitor" was not in the description.
- Confirmed: Semm 12 Sep 1980, Mühe 12 Sep 1985 and 97 by March 1987, "Mickey Mouse surgery," Mouret 17 March 1987, Perissat 1989 videotape, Mühe's 1992 honor, da Vinci FDA 2000.
- Residual doubt: both Mühe sources trace to Litynski's interviews with Mühe, so the detail rests on one author's work.

## cochlear-implant: corrected
- Body said House and Doyle implanted a single electrode "in a deaf patient" in 1961 without qualification; the sources say two patients in 1961, with the 9 January date from the Cureus House biography. Kept the date and the single-patient reading of that day.
- Deaf-community sentence was stated as fact ("many opposed implanting children"). The only source is an autobiographical case report in Cureus, which describes what the community believes. Reworded to match it and the confidence note now says it rests on a secondary account.
- Note also records that the Lasker Foundation dates the first multichannel FDA approval to 1985, while a Journal of Medicine and Life review says Cochlear Ltd introduced the first multichannel system in 1984.
- Confirmed: 1957 Djourno and Eyriès, 3M/House device first FDA-approved in 1984, Hochmair December 1977 and Clark August 1978.
- Residual doubt: a stronger source on the Deaf-community debate (for example a position statement) could not be fetched.

## deep-brain-stimulation: corrected
- Summary and body said "adjustable"; the Lasker source supports "reversible by reducing stimulation." Changed to "reversible."
- Removed the confidence-note claim that "one review dates the Parkinson's approval to 2001": no such review was found. FDA records confirm 31 July 1997 (tremor) and 14 January 2002 (Parkinson's).
- Added that deep brain stimulation was tried before 1987 under other names (Hariz et al. cite Bechtereva and Cooper), so Grenoble is described as the start of the modern era, not the first.
- Removed "returned when the current went off" (not found in the cited text). "Monkey studies" now "animal work" (Lasker describes DeLong's monkey experiments, but the body drew on more than that).
- Confirmed: just below 100 Hz the tremor stopped, 1987 in Grenoble, STN from 1993.

- Added that Endo independently isolated the compound Merck's team identified as lovastatin (Lasker), with a quote. "Chemist" became "biochemist"; "controls cholesterol production" became "a key enzyme in" it.

## human-genome-project: corrected

- The Celera sentence was stated as fact. Its source is a book review by Wellcome's Michael Morgan, a partisan insider, so the body now attributes it to him and says "a number of genes."
- "Last gaps closed only in 2022" in the note and "the Y chromosome later still" in the myth box overreached: T2T-CHM13 lacks Y and no cited source reports Y being completed. Both fixed.
- Image caption said ABI machines "read DNA in the early genome era"; unsupported. The Commons description gives only the model name, photo date (2012), and licence (CC BY-SA 3.0). Caption now says that.
- Confirmed: 90 percent draft in June 2000, 92 percent and under 400 gaps in 2003, $3 billion projected, $300 million for the draft, under $1,500 in late 2015, 20,000–25,000 genes, T2T 31 March 2022 with about 200 million bases and 99 protein-coding genes.
