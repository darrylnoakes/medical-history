# Verification, batch 08c

Second-pass fact-check of seven entries. All source URLs were re-opened or re-read from saved copies, and the quotes were machine-checked.

## laennec-stethoscope: corrected
- Date confidence was `firm`, but the 1816 paper-tube episode rests only on Laennec's own account in the 1819 treatise, written about three years later. A modern summary adds "fall of 1816". Changed `confidence.date` to `agreed`, added a narrow range (1816–1817) and rewrote the note.
- Body said "Over the next three years" and "named it the stethoscope". The sources give neither the span nor a single act of naming. Laennec's own text says he "commonly" called the instrument "the Cylinder, sometimes the Stethoscope", and a secondary source says he agonized over a name. Now "Over the following years" and "called it".
- Body implied that Forbes's 1821 translation caused the instrument's spread. No source says so. Reworded to say the book caused little stir and the instrument "gradually won acceptance".
- Cut "the disease he had studied most closely" (unsupported). Changed Forbes from "English" to "British physician". Softened the xrays link note to "nearly eighty years".
- Dropped the Berezutskyi letter (PMC13277514). It is a weak letter to the editor with odd claims, and it backed nothing in the body that the other sources did not. Five sources remain.
- Image added: Laennec's 1819 drawings of the stethoscope (public domain).
- Residual doubt: the anecdote is Laennec's own, and the "shyness" version is a gloss on it.

## beaumont: corrected
- Body was about 125 words, over the tier-3 cap of 100. Rewritten shorter.
- "An eighteen-year-old" became "young". Beaumont says "about eighteen", but Osler reports St. Martin was 83 at death in 1880, which does not fit. The confidence note now says so.
- "Showing that digestion was chemical" softened to "helping to show". The source says Beaumont "helped to experimentally establish" it.
- The note said St. Martin was "poor and unable to write". Only "signed with a mark" (letters written for him) is sourced, so "poor" was cut.
- The tuskegee link note ("far graver") was editorializing and is now neutral.
- Residual doubt: the contracts Osler describes date from 1833, so "bound by contract" covers the later years, not the whole 1825–1833 span.

## anatomy-act: corrected
- "Only executed murderers could legally be dissected" was too strong. The Shaw source (PMC11825383) records that courts also treated post-mortem bodies from coroners' inquests as a lawful supply. Now "largely limited to", and the note says so.
- "In Edinburgh in 1828 ... killing" changed to "tried in 1828". The sources date the trial, not the killing period.
- Residual doubt: "at least 16" is the figure the sources give. The Murder Act is dated 1751 or 1752 depending on the source (noted in the confidence note).

## cell-theory: corrected
- The myth record said cells "crystallized out of a fluid". The sources support "free cell formation" in a structureless fluid (cytoblastema). Schwann's crystallization analogy is about organisms in general. Reworded.
- "A reasonable reading of the microscopes of the day" was the writer's gloss. Replaced with the sourced point that Schwann's terms were close to Müller's descriptions of formative fluid.
- The microscope link note ("seen and named since the 1660s") was not sourced here and was replaced.
- Image added: Plate I of Schwann's 1839 book, drawn by Schwann (CC BY, Wellcome).
- Residual doubt: Virchow's slogan is not original to him in some accounts. Not sourced here, so left out.

## chadwick: corrected (minor)
- Verified against the report text: the conclusions on atmospheric impurities, the war comparison, "no exemptions" for prosperity and wages, and drainage, refuse removal and water as the primary measures are all there. The 1800 birth date, the New Poor Law role and the 1848 Act are sourced.
- "Cholera and typhoid spread through contaminated water" softened to "largely". The links to snow-cholera and nightingale were reworded. "Showed" became "pointed to". "Believed in miasma" is not sourced here, so it became "another sanitary reformer of the same era".
- Image added: a map of Leeds from the report's evidence (the report says Baker prepared it at Chadwick's request). Licence CC BY, Wellcome.

## blackwell: corrected
- "Every school ... had turned her down" was an inference. NLM says she applied to all of them and Geneva accepted her in 1847. Reworded to that.
- "Not the first woman to practise medicine with a degree" became "hold a medical degree". the source claims are about degrees (Erxleben 1754, Barry 1812), so the text now says "hold a medical degree".
- The 1876 Act claim is as the Collins source states it (examinations "must be allowed"). Standard accounts say the Act was permissive, enabling bodies to admit women. The body's wording ("opened") fits both, and the note flags it as secondary. Left as a residual doubt.
- Removed the unsourced "women had long worked as healers" from the myth record. Corrected the Barry and Crumpler life details in `people` from the sources.
- Image added: NLM portrait of Elizabeth Blackwell, public domain. Avoided another Commons file titled "Blackwell Elisabeth", which shows a different, 18th-century woman.

## hypodermic-syringe: corrected
- Body was about 125 words, over the cap. Rewritten.
- The myth said Wood's wife was "the first person addicted to injected morphine". No source says "addicted". Paladini says she was "the first person to be administered morphine injections". The tradition is restated accordingly. Wood's own paper describes an old unmarried woman ("Miss ..."). It also says she "never wakened" until next morning and was hard to rouse, so "slept deeply" replaces "slept through the night".
- The opioid-crisis link ("fears about dependence began with the syringe") overreached. It now cites only Allbutt's 1870 warning.
- Residual doubt: Rynd's birth year appears as 1801 and 1811 in different sources; only the death year (1861) is consistent. The entry uses 1801.
