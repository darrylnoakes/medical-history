# Verification, batch 07b

All 30 sources fetch and every quote is found (`check-sources`); `validate-content` is clean. Europe PMC API URLs were replaced by `pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC<id>/` pages. Commons file pages were checked for all six images (some API calls were rate-limited, so the Hooke and Charles II files were checked on retry).

## plague-doctor-costume: corrected
- Body said "firm evidence comes from Rome in 1656: Bartholin described...". Ruisinger dates Bartholin's book to 1661; the 1656 epidemic is what it describes. Reworded.
- The de Lorme story was called "a later account"; Live Science says de Lorme himself wrote of it in a mid-17th-century work, and Ruisinger only says the idea is "said to" go back to him. Now "dates from the mid-17th century" and "said to have designed". Traditional-date note and confidence note changed to match.
- Myth record and confidence note said the German masks "show signs of never having been worn". Ruisinger says only that details speak against use as protective clothing and that they may be historicizing copies or forgeries. Softened.
- Summary "little sign that many doctors ever wore one" changed to "widely worn". Caption now adds Nuremberg and Fürst (British Museum description confirms). Bartholin's role dated to 1661 in the people line.
- Residual doubt: Live Science is a news source for the 1619 and Fürst details; the Münster page and Ruisinger are the strong sources.

## harvey: corrected
- "Studied at Padua under Fabricius" is stronger than the source, which says Fabricius was professor while Harvey studied there. Reworded in body and people line.
- "Louis XIV had been bled 38 times" half a century after Harvey's death: National Geographic says 38 bloodlettings over his whole life (plus hundreds of enemas, about 2,000 purgatives). Rewritten.
- Confidence note tightened. About 70 pages, 1618 royal physician, 1661 capillaries, Frankfurt 1628, "more than one thousand beats" and the half-ounce/drachm arithmetic all confirmed. Image file is a Wellcome plate of forearm valves (description just "Valves in forearm"); the caption's explanation is Harvey's own experiment.
- Residual doubt: "widely accepted by 1657" rests on National Geographic alone.

## graunt: corrected
- "Elderly women called searchers": Graunt calls them "antient Matrons, sworn to their office". Quoted accordingly. "Parish clerks printed the totals" changed to compiled (the Company of Parish Clerks published).
- "Mainly from the annual summaries of 1604–1660" is not in the sources; Connor says annual summaries began in 1604. Range cut.
- The ale detail was "bribed with ale"; Graunt says a cup of ale and a double fee. Fixed.
- Myth record said Graunt "was the first to describe, with many assumptions, the dying-out of a group of births", an unearned first. Rewritten to what Connor supports (not a modern life table; Halley's 1693 table was the first from real ages at death). Charles II's recommendation is now "according to later accounts" (Connor and the encyclopedia assert it, without a primary record).
- Petty "coined" political arithmetic changed to "labeled" (encyclopedia wording).
- Residual doubt: 36 percent (32 percent excluding stillbirths and abortions) is the source's figure for deaths before six; "about a third" is fair.

## willis: corrected (minor)
- Birth details differ between sources (21 vs 27 January 1621; Great Bedwyn vs a farm near Oxford); the confidence note said only birthplace. Fixed.
- "Wren drew illustrations" now "drafted" (Caron). Added life dates for Wren and Lower (from the sources).
- The Sydenham link called him Willis's "London rival"; no source says rival. Reworded as a contrast.
- Amsterdam 1664 edition in the image caption confirmed by the Commons description (Schagen, 1664; Wren credited in the preface). Note the Amsterdam printing is a second edition; the first was London 1664.

## microscope: confirmed, one wording fix
- "First detailed description of protists and bacteria" is Lane's judgment; now attributed to "a modern historian". Hooke/Pepys/satirist, 500+ instruments, 200x vs 20-30x, Huygens, Grew, Hooke's third attempt in 1677, eight testimonies, Kircher, Varro-era antiquity of the idea, and miasma until the 19th century all located in the sources.
- Myth box kept: Münster page documents the long history of the idea and Kircher; the "tradition" is a simplified version of Latour's provocation, so it is the weakest myth box in the batch.
- Residual doubt: "1683 similar creatures in dental plaque" rests on UCMP (the letter is dated 17 September 1683).

## blood-transfusion-1667: substantially rewritten
- Title "first blood transfusions" claimed priority; retitled "Early blood transfusions".
- "Transfusion between humans came only in 1818 ... for bleeding after childbirth" was wrong in detail: Chandler et al. say Blundell's first case (September 1818) was a man with stomach cancer, with postpartum cases later. Now "first documented human-to-human transfusion".
- Added missing context from a new source (J Vasc Surg 2012, PMC4065715): Lower's February 1665 dog experiment, Denis's first human case on 15 June 1667 (lamb blood), Lower's Coga transfusion on 23 November 1667, and Denis's manslaughter trial (cleared).
- Pepys's frantic former minister now quoted as "been a kind of minister"; "volunteer" changed to paid subject. Summary changed to match. Body shortened to fit the tier.
- Residual doubt: Pepys reports the 14 November 1666 dog experiment at second hand (from Dr Croone). Whether King took part in the Coga transfusion is from the Red Cross page; Chandler names only Lower.

Willis and Ramazzini link notes adjusted (Paladino et al. do say Ramazzini's neo-Hippocratism was inspired by Sydenham, so that link stands).

## royal-touch: corrected
- Date range ran only to 1682 although the body and summary use 1684; now 1660-1685 (reign). Display changed.
- Myth box removed: its only support was a 19th-century editor's footnote ("no direct evidence of the early Norman kings"), which modern scholarship may have superseded; not safe as a "record".
- "Without effect" for Johnson now attributed to Boswell (text: "This touch, however, was without any effect"). The 1684 trampling is credited to Macaulay and flagged as the only source. Summary changed from "some 90,000" to a 90,000-100,000 range. Johnson's dates added (Boswell text).
- Residual doubt: both numbers are secondhand (Burn 1862 via Wheatley; Macaulay 1855). A modern study of the touch (Bloch, Brogan) should replace Macaulay when available.

- "Crude earplugs" for millers: the source (1713 edition) recommends cotton in the ears of coppersmiths, not millers. Corrected.
- Summary "made him the father of occupational medicine" is the Collegium Ramazzini's phrasing ("considered"); softened. "Midwives worn out" replaced with kneeling for hours.
- Added the 1700 move to Padua and that the kneeling applied to Italian midwives. Sydenham link note now matches the source.
